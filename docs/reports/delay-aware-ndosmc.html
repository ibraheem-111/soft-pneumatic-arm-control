Delay-Aware NDOSMC Explained

This controller tracked the scored 7 mm circle to about 0.1 mm once past the start. Here it is written out step by step, each equation next to what it means. It is Qiao, Tao & Zhang's sliding-mode controller with a disturbance observer, extended so that it knows about the half-second the arm takes to respond.

Code: DelayAwareNDOSMC in scripts/ndosmc.py and Section 14 of notebooks/SMC_NDOSMC_delay_aware.ipynb. Model: docs/reports/task-space-model.html. Results: docs/reports/controller-comparison.html.

The problem, and the shower analogy

A pressure command does not reach the arm straight away. It waits 0.5 s in the regulator and tubing, and then the pressure creeps in with a 0.6 s time constant. Only after that does the arm bend, which it does quickly.

It is like a shower with a long pipe. Turn the hot tap and nothing happens for half a second; then the temperature drifts in. React only to how warm the water feels now and you keep over-turning the tap, so the temperature swings back and forth. Someone who knows the shower does four things instead:

  1. Remembers how much they have already turned the tap. That hot water is on its way. (steps ①–②)
  2. Aims at the temperature they will want by the time it arrives. (③)
  3. Nudges the tap a little extra at first, because the heat comes in slowly. (⑧)
  4. Fine-tunes whatever still feels off. (④–⑥)

The controller does exactly these four things.

What it reads, and what it sends

Reads, every 10 ms

  • Tip position from motion capture (obs["tip_pos"]), and the settled tip once, as "home".
  • The reference the evaluation hands it for this step.
  • Its own past commands, which it keeps for the internal copy.

It does not use velocity, the pressure sensors, joint angles, true pressures or any simulator parameter. Its model (T, τ, g, a, H) was identified beforehand from tip and pressure-sensor data only, with noise added, on seeds other than the scored one.

Sends: 3 pressures

One absolute pressure each for segments S2, S3 and S4, in 0–9 psi. That is all the calibrated plant accepts: S1 is a sealed reservoir and each segment's five pouches share one regulator. A 20-pouch version for make_sim() would need a different allocation (⑦) and that plant has no 0.5 s dead time, so step ② would shrink to the lag alone.

How the pieces connect

Step by step: the equation and what it means

Per axis \(i \in \{x, y, z\}\). \(X\) is the tip position minus the settled pose (mm), \(u\) the three segment commands (psi), \(T = 0.5\) s, \(\tau = 0.6\) s, regulator gains \(g = (1.12, 1.10, 1.16)\), \(a = (4.77, 4.70, 15.5)\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). All of these were identified from sensor data.

Model of the arm (M2)
\[ \tau\,\dot{\bar p}_j = -\bar p_j + \big(u_j(t-T) - 4.5\big),\qquad \Delta p_j = g_j\,\bar p_j \]
\[ \dot X_i = -a_i X_i + a_i (H\,\Delta p)_i + d_i \]

The command first waits \(T\), then the pressure rises toward it with a lag \(\tau\). The pressure bends the arm, which on its own springs back toward rest at rate \(a\). \(H\) says how each segment's pressure moves x, y and z. \(d\) is everything the model leaves out: residual inertia, Coriolis, the sealed reservoir's slow leak.

① Internal copy, no delay
\[ \tau\,\dot{\hat p} = -\hat p + g\,\big(u(t) - 4.5\big),\qquad \dot{\hat X} = -a\hat X + aH\hat p \]

A small simulator of the arm runs inside the controller, but it feels each command immediately. At any moment it shows what the real arm is about to do.

② Predictor (Smith)
\[ \tilde X(t) = X(t) + \big[\hat X(t) - \hat X(t-T)\big] \;\approx\; X(t+T) \]

Where the tip is now, plus how far it will still move because of commands already sent but not yet arrived. The sum is where the tip will be in half a second. Because \(X(t)\) is measured, any error in the copy shows up here automatically and gets corrected.

③ Aim ahead
\[ \tilde r = r + T\dot r + \tfrac12 T^2\ddot r,\qquad \dot{\tilde r} \approx \dot r + T\ddot r \]

We now steer the future tip, so we compare it with the future target. \(\dot r\) and \(\ddot r\) come from the last few reference samples. On a smooth circle this short extrapolation is accurate.

④ The error to remove
\[ \sigma_i = \tilde X_i - \tilde r_i \]

The sliding variable is simply the predicted error. The model is first order, so the paper's surface is the error itself; no derivative is needed.

⑤ Disturbance observer (paper eqs. 6–8)
\[ \hat d = z + l_1\tilde X + l_2\tilde X\lvert\tilde X\rvert,\qquad g_o = l_1 + 2l_2\lvert\tilde X\rvert \]
\[ \dot z = g_o\,\big(a\tilde X - a v - \hat d\big) \]

A running estimate of "the push I did not model". It compares how the tip actually moved with how the model said it would and treats the gap as a disturbance. Here it changes the result by only about 0.02 mm, because the predictor already feeds model errors back.

⑥ Sliding-mode law (paper eq. 12)
\[ v_i = \frac{1}{a_i}\Big[\dot{\tilde r}_i + a_i\tilde X_i - \lambda\,\sigma_i - \eta_i\,\mathrm{sat}(\sigma_i/\mu) - \hat d_i\Big] \]
\[ \eta_i = \Phi\,e^{-g_o t} + \epsilon/g_o \]

−λσ: a spring pulling the error to zero. With λ = 4 s⁻¹ it halves roughly every 0.17 s. \(\dot{\tilde r}\): move along with the target instead of waiting for an error. \(+a\tilde X\): the arm springs back by itself, so push a bit extra to cancel that. −η sat: an extra, bounded push when the model is off, softened inside a 1 mm band so the valves don't chatter. −\(\hat d\): subtract the observer's estimate.

⑦ Mix into three segments
\[ p_{\mathrm{des}} = H^{-1} v \]

Like a mixing desk: "move x, y and z this much" becomes three segment pressures. S2 pushes +y, S3 pushes −x, S4 pushes −y, and raising all three together stretches the arm (z).

⑧ Beat the lag, then clip
\[ u = \mathrm{clip}\!\Big(4.5 + \frac{p_{\mathrm{des}} + \tau\,\dot p_{\mathrm{des}}}{g},\ 0,\ 9\Big) \]

Pressure rises sluggishly, so ask for more than you want while the target is changing, in proportion to how fast it changes. It is like pressing the accelerator harder at first and easing off once the car is moving. Dividing by \(g\) undoes the regulator gain, and the clip keeps the command within 0–9 psi.

Why the law in ⑥ is the right one

Ignoring the delay, which steps ② and ③ have already moved out of the way, substitute ⑥ into the model:

\[ \dot\sigma = \dot{\tilde X} - \dot{\tilde r} = -a\tilde X + \big(\dot{\tilde r} + a\tilde X - \lambda\sigma - \eta\,\mathrm{sat}(\sigma/\mu) - \hat d\big) + d - \dot{\tilde r} = -\lambda\sigma - \eta\,\mathrm{sat}(\sigma/\mu) + (d - \hat d). \]

Every known effect cancels. What remains is an error that decays like \(e^{-\lambda t}\), a bounded push against what is unknown, and the observer removing most of that unknown. With \(V = \tfrac12\sigma^2\), the same argument as the paper's eq. 13 gives \(\dot V \le -2\lambda V\) once \(\eta\) exceeds the observer error.

Why it beats the paper's version, and where it can fail

Why it works

The paper assumes pressure arrives instantly. Its observer then sees the controller's own late commands as a disturbance and always corrects 0.5 s too late, so it keeps chasing. Steps ② and ⑧ account for the half second explicitly, so the main law never has to fight it.

Where it can fail

  • Wrong delay. A Smith predictor relies on \(T\). If the real delay drifts from 0.5 s the prediction is off; this was not tested.
  • Fast surprises. A sudden disturbance can only be corrected after it has passed through the delay.
  • Saturation. On the faster, larger held-out circle the commands sat at 0 or 9 psi about 21 % of the time.
  • Simulation only. The real regulator may not be a clean delay plus one lag.
